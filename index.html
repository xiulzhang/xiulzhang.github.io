About

I study how identity, violence, and foreign engagement shape political accountability in Africa: whom citizens vote for, whom they blame, and whom they trust.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Government and Politics at the University of Maryland, College Park. My research combines original survey, vignette, and conjoint experiments in Nigeria with geocoded conflict data and cross-national surveys from across Africa, and with computational text methods applied to African political speech and media. I hold an M.A. in African Studies from UCLA and a B.A. in International Politics and Economics from Peking University. Before graduate school I worked on an agricultural technology project at the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture in Kigali and as an analyst at a risk consultancy in Shanghai.

I am on the 2026-27 academic job market.

Job market paper

Whose Ethnicity Counts? Gender, Patriarchy, and Coethnic Voting in Nigeria

Decades of research in comparative political behavior show that voters favor coethnic candidates, yet we know little about women's place in ethnic politics. I argue that female candidates face distinct disadvantages in political arenas where ethnic identity is politically salient yet ethnic ascription is structurally patriarchal. Where patrilineal conventions establish men as the primary anchors of ethnic membership, women's ethnic ties carry less weight in establishing coethnic status, leaving them with less ethnic capital to draw upon for electoral support. I examine these dynamics across two crucial dimensions of ethnic ascription: lineage and marriage. Drawing on two conjoint experiments and qualitative fieldwork in Lagos, Nigeria, I show that paternal ethnic ties confer substantially greater electoral returns than maternal ones, regardless of candidate gender. In marriage, male candidates retain coethnic support when marrying outside their group and even gain support from their wives' communities. Female candidates, however, lose support from their ethnic communities of origin without gaining equivalent support from their husbands' communities. Crucially, this penalty holds even among female voters who otherwise favor female candidates. These findings highlight how patriarchal rules of ethnic ascription translate into distinct electoral disadvantages for women.

Job market paper

The team behind two thousand doorsteps

The conjoint experiments in my job market paper were not run online. Enumerators carried tablets through neighborhoods across Lagos State, starting from a randomly chosen point, knocking on every fifth door, and reading candidate profiles aloud to 2,117 adults between August and October 2026. Before any of that could happen, this team spent days in training arguing with me about wording: how to describe a candidate's mother's ethnicity so that a respondent hears it as ordinary biography rather than as a strange question.

Those arguments shaped the design more than any referee has. The finding that a father's ethnicity carries more electoral weight than a mother's came out of their doorsteps, and so did the enumerator fixed effects in every table.

Programmatic goods and identity

The city is yours

Lagos is a city of arrivals. People from every part of Nigeria come to make a living there, and the sign says what the city promises them. My research asks what that promise means in practice: whether a politician's ethnicity still decides who is believed to deserve a health center or a cash transfer, and whether clear eligibility rules can loosen that grip.

The evening debriefs over football and takeaway are where fieldwork actually happens. This is where I learned which questions respondents laughed at, which ones made them uncomfortable, and which programs they had seen promised and never delivered. Several items in the survey were rewritten after nights like this one.

China, the United States, and Nigeria

Getting things done

The railway between Lagos and Ibadan was built by a Chinese contractor and is wrapped, inside and out, in Nigerian life: instant noodle advertisements, a celebration of Ibadan, families with luggage heading home. Riding it is the closest thing to a field visit for the question John McCauley and I ask in our Nigeria surveys. When citizens of the Global South compare the major powers, do they reward the one whose values they admire, or the one that shows up and builds?

Our respondents say both, in different registers. The United States inspires aspirations; China is credited with getting things done. The paper on project characteristics follows up by asking which features of a foreign-funded project, from who builds it to who benefits, move Nigerians' support, independently of the flag on the sign.
