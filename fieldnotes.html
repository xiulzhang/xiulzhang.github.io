Job market paper

The team behind two thousand doorsteps

The conjoint experiments in my job market paper were not run online. Enumerators carried tablets through neighborhoods across Lagos State, starting from a randomly chosen point, knocking on every fifth door, and reading candidate profiles aloud to 2,117 adults between August and October 2026. Before any of that could happen, this team spent days in training arguing with me about wording: how to describe a candidate's mother's ethnicity so that a respondent hears it as ordinary biography rather than as a strange question.

Those arguments shaped the design more than any referee has. The finding that a father's ethnicity carries more electoral weight than a mother's came out of their doorsteps, and so did the enumerator fixed effects in every table.

Programmatic goods and identity

The city is yours

Lagos is a city of arrivals. People from every part of Nigeria come to make a living there, and the sign says what the city promises them. My research asks what that promise means in practice: whether a politician's ethnicity still decides who is believed to deserve a health center or a cash transfer, and whether clear eligibility rules can loosen that grip.

The evening debriefs over football and takeaway are where fieldwork actually happens. This is where I learned which questions respondents laughed at, which ones made them uncomfortable, and which programs they had seen promised and never delivered. Several items in the survey were rewritten after nights like this one.

China, the United States, and Nigeria

Getting things done

The railway between Lagos and Ibadan was built by a Chinese contractor and is wrapped, inside and out, in Nigerian life: instant noodle advertisements, a celebration of Ibadan, families with luggage heading home. Riding it is the closest thing to a field visit for the question John McCauley and I ask in our Nigeria surveys. When citizens of the Global South compare the major powers, do they reward the one whose values they admire, or the one that shows up and builds?

Our respondents say both, in different registers. The United States inspires aspirations; China is credited with getting things done. The paper on project characteristics follows up by asking which features of a foreign-funded project, from who builds it to who benefits, move Nigerians' support, independently of the flag on the sign.
